# India's Defence, Decoded — Episode 5: The Forty-Year Jet

**Working title:** *Why India's Own Fighter Took Forty Years | Tejas Mk1A*
**In three words:** "DESIGN · BUILD · POWER" · **Blueprint wall:** Tejas pinned as the fifth card · **As of:** 19 November 2026 · **Refresh by:** June 2027 (the first Mk1A handover, Aero India 2027, the Dubai court of inquiry, or any Mk2 first flight)

**Length:** about 17 and a half minutes, bilingual (English and Hindi audio tracks). English narration about 2,297 words. This is the longest episode in the series so far — the engine act is about three and a half minutes of it. If a shorter cut is wanted, the Mark 1A, squadron-maths and debate beats can each lose a third without losing an argument.

**The episode's argument:** India can design a fighter. India can build a fighter. What India cannot yet do is power one. Every delay in this programme, across forty years, traces back to the one component the country has never managed to make — and that is why finished aircraft and a signed contract are not the same thing as a squadron. The episode does not end there: the last act is the engine India is building now, and what it would actually take to finish it.

**Safety and accuracy notes:**
- **No maps.** Bases are named in text only; nothing is plotted.
- **Delivery status is dated throughout** ("as of 19 November 2026"), because this is the fastest-moving episode in the series so far. If a handover is announced before the render, the cold open, the engine segment and the status rows all change.
- **The Dubai Airshow crash is handled with restraint.** The pilot is named once, with his rank, because the Indian Air Force named him. No footage, no reconstruction, no silhouette of the aircraft in distress — only a dated line of text. The court of inquiry had not reported publicly as of 19 November 2026, so no cause is stated or hinted at. Nothing in the episode links the crash to the delays.
- **Reported figures are labelled as reported.** HAL's "ten aircraft built" and the engine counts come from press reporting, not from a government release, and the narration says so.
- **The debate is given both sides** on equal cards. Neither side is about whether the aircraft is good.
- **No logos, no flags, no real likenesses.** Officials are named in text where quoted.

| 2:19–3:39 | 1983: why start at all | A blueprint sheet headed AUGUST 1983 · PROJECT DEFINITION · ₹560 CRORE. Beside it, a row of small aircraft silhouettes labelled MiG-21 fades one by one. | Start with why. By the early 1980s the Indian Air Force's backbone was the MiG-21. Hundreds of them, Soviet-designed, licence-built in India, and getting old. Something had to replace them, and India had two choices: buy the next aircraft from abroad, as it had always done, or learn to make one. In August 1983 the government sanctioned the Light Combat Aircraft programme, with five hundred and sixty crore rupees for the project definition phase alone. A new organisation, the Aeronautical Development Agency, was created to design it, with Hindustan Aeronautics Limited as the partner who would build it. Now, be clear about what was being attempted. India had licence-built other people's aircraft for decades. This was different. This was a clean-sheet, unstable, fly-by-wire, composite-heavy fighter, in a country that had never designed a supersonic combat aircraft and had very little of the industry you need to do it — no modern avionics base, no composites industry at scale, no engine house with a working military turbofan. The first flight was targeted for 1994. |
| 3:39–4:59 | 1998: the embargo | A dotted line on the blueprint snaps. Two cards print and then grey out, labelled FOREIGN PARTNERS · ENGINEERS WITHDRAWN, with the date MAY 1998. A clock face beside them winds backwards. | And then, in May 1998, India tested nuclear weapons at Pokhran, and the United States imposed a technology embargo. Lockheed Martin and General Electric had both agreed to lend expertise to the programme. Their engineers went home. One Indian account of that period says the withdrawal turned the clock back by four years. Now, you can read that two ways, and both are true. It was a serious setback, and the first flight, already a decade late, slipped further. It was also the moment the programme stopped being able to outsource its hardest problems. The flight control work had to be finished in India, by Indian engineers, because there was no longer anyone else to finish it. India now has that capability permanently. It was bought at a cost nobody would have chosen to pay. The aircraft finally flew on the fourth of January 2001 — eighteen years after the programme began, and seven years after the date it was meant to fly. In 2003 the Prime Minister, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, gave it a name: Tejas. |
| 4:59–6:24 | Flying is not serving | A horizontal timeline draws itself, markers printing in one at a time: 2001 FIRST FLIGHT · 2011 IOC · DEC 2013 IOC-II · FEB 2019 FOC · 2016 No. 45 SQN · 2020 No. 18 SQN. | Here is the thing most people get wrong about aircraft programmes. A first flight is not the finish line. It is roughly the halfway mark. Between a prototype in the air and a squadron on alert sit thousands of test flights, weapons trials, and two certificates. Initial Operational Clearance says the aircraft is safe and useful enough for the Air Force to fly properly; Tejas got a first one in 2011 and a fuller one in December 2013. Final Operational Clearance says it can do everything it was specified to do — all the weapons, air-to-air refuelling, the full envelope. That came at Aero India in February 2019, years late. In between, on the first of July 2016, the Air Force stood up Number 45 Squadron, the Flying Daggers, as the first Tejas unit, and in 2020 Number 18 Squadron, the Flying Bullets, became the second. Forty aircraft were ordered in that first batch. As of September 2025, reporting said all thirty-two fighters and six of the eight trainers had been delivered — an order placed in the 2000s, still being closed out two decades later. |
| 6:24–7:50 | What the Mark 1A actually is | The fighter outline redraws, with four callouts printing in turn: AESA RADAR · ELECTRONIC WARFARE SUITE · BEYOND-VISUAL-RANGE MISSILE · AIR-TO-AIR REFUELLING. Then two contract cards land side by side: 83 JETS · 3 FEB 2021 · ₹48,000 CR and 97 JETS · 25 SEP 2025 · ₹62,370 CR, with a total beneath: 180. | So what is the Mark 1A? Not a new aircraft. The same airframe, with the parts that decide a modern air battle replaced. The Press Information Bureau lists four: an AESA radar — an electronically scanned radar with no moving parts, which can track many targets at once and is much harder to jam; an electronic warfare suite; a beyond-visual-range missile, so it can shoot at something it has not yet seen with its own eyes; and air-to-air refuelling, which turns a short-legged aircraft into one that can stay up. That 2021 contract was for seventy-three Mark 1A fighters and ten trainers. Then, on the twenty-fifth of September 2025, the Defence Ministry signed a second contract with HAL: ninety-seven more aircraft, sixty-eight single-seat and twenty-nine twin-seat, for over sixty-two thousand three hundred and seventy crore rupees. Indigenous content above sixty-four per cent, and this time including India's own Uttam AESA radar and the Swayam Raksha Kavach self-protection suite in place of imported ones. Add the two contracts together and the order book is a hundred and eighty aircraft. On paper, that is around ten squadrons of Indian-built fighters. |
| 7:50–9:02 | The engine India buys | The turbine icon from the anchor returns, still an outline. Two number cards print: ORDERED JAN 2021 · 99 ENGINES · DUE FROM APRIL 2023 · 16/YEAR and DELIVERED BY OCT 2025 · 4. Then a second contract card: 7 NOV 2025 · 113 MORE ENGINES · DELIVERY 2027–2032. | Which brings us to the fourth problem, the one India did not solve. The Tejas is powered by the General Electric F404, an American engine. In January 2021 HAL ordered ninety-nine of them, for about seven hundred and sixteen million dollars, with deliveries due to begin in April 2023 at sixteen a year. The first one arrived in March 2025, two years late. The Tribune reported that as of October 2025, four engines had been delivered. Four. Against a plan of sixteen a year. That is the entire reason an aircraft contracted in 2021 had not been handed over by late 2026 — not the airframe, not the software, not HAL's factory floor. In November 2025 HAL signed for a hundred and thirteen more F404s for the second batch of jets, to be delivered between 2027 and 2032. So the question everybody asks, and it is the right question: why does India not simply build its own? |
| 9:02–10:17 | Kaveri, and why engines are the hard part | A timeline strip: KAVERI · DELINKED 2008 · ABOUT 72 kN vs 83–85 NEEDED. Then the hollow turbine from the anchor is cut away, and each stage fills as it is named: COMPRESSOR · COMBUSTOR · TURBINE. A temperature tag prints beside the hot section: GAS HOTTER THAN THE BLADE'S MELTING POINT. | It tried. DRDO's Gas Turbine Research Establishment spent decades on an engine called Kaveri. Reports put the thrust it reached at around seventy-two kilonewtons, against the eighty-three to eighty-five the aircraft needed, and in 2008 Kaveri was formally delinked from the fighter programme. That is usually where the story gets told as a national failure. It is worth understanding why it is harder than that. A jet engine is probably the hardest object a country can manufacture. Air goes in the front, a compressor squeezes it to many times atmospheric pressure, fuel burns, and the gas leaves through a turbine. The gas hitting those turbine blades is hotter than the temperature at which the blades themselves would melt. They survive because each blade is grown as a single crystal of nickel alloy, coated in ceramic, and cooled by air bled through tiny holes inside it — and because it has to do all that, spinning, for thousands of hours, without failing once. |
| 10:17–11:20 | And yet | A zoomed outline of a four-engine transport aircraft with one engine position highlighted, labelled FLYING TESTBED. Cards print: 1 OCT 2026 · KAVERI DERIVATIVE ENGINE · 48.5 kN DRY · TARGET 46 · FOR GHATAK UCAV. | Then you need the places to prove it: high-altitude test beds, a flying testbed, a certification chain. India had almost none of that when Kaveri began. And yet. On the first of October 2026 it was reported that the Kaveri Derivative Engine — the version without an afterburner — had been tested in Russia, on the ground and then in the air, on a modified Il-76 flying testbed. It produced a peak dry thrust of forty-eight point five kilonewtons against a target of forty-six, and it is meant for the Ghatak unmanned combat aircraft, with certification and integration still ahead. That is not an engine for Tejas. But it is an Indian-designed military turbofan that flew and beat its own target, and that is a different sentence from the one India has been writing for thirty years. |
| 11:20–12:39 | Three tracks | Three parallel tracks draw downward from a single point, filling one at a time: LICENCE-BUILD F414 · CO-DEVELOP 120 kN · BUILD OUR OWN. A question mark sits at the bottom where they meet. | So the ambition now runs on three tracks at once. One: licence-build. The GE F414, the engine chosen for Tejas Mark 2, is to be made in India by HAL under a reported eighty per cent technology transfer. That gives India the factory — though defence sources are clear that building someone else's engine is not the same as being able to design one. Two: co-develop. For the fifth-generation AMCA, India wants a new engine of a hundred and twenty kilonewtons or more, designed jointly with a foreign partner. Rolls-Royce and Safran are the two left in the running, and Rolls-Royce's pitch, reported in June 2026, offers full technology transfer with the intellectual property staying in India, a core test by 2030 and production by 2036. No partner has been announced. Three: build our own, from the bottom up, starting with a drone engine that works. Watch which of those three the government actually signs. That choice decides whether India owns a jet engine in the 2030s, or rents one for another generation. |
| 12:39–13:40 | Why the delay actually hurts | A bar of squadron blocks draws in: 42.5 SANCTIONED as a faint outline, and a shorter solid bar beneath it. A figure prints: ABOUT 29–31. Two blocks labelled MiG-21 fade out. | Now, why does any of this matter beyond a factory schedule? Because of arithmetic. The Indian Air Force is authorised to hold forty-two and a half fighter squadrons. In September 2025, reporting put the actual number at thirty-one, falling to twenty-nine as two MiG-21 squadrons retired. The MiG-21 that Tejas was conceived in 1983 to replace has now left service entirely. Tejas was supposed to arrive as those aircraft left. Instead the old aircraft went first and the new ones are late, and the gap between the two is the squadron shortfall you read about. Every month of engine delay is a month that gap stays open. And it is worth saying plainly: that is not an argument against building your own. It is an argument about what it costs to start late. |

| 14:31–15:45 | Where people disagree | Two equal cards side by side, headed THE CASE FOR PATIENCE and THE CASE FOR URGENCY, filling in line by line. | And this is where informed people genuinely disagree. The case for patience: every country that builds fighters took decades and had its own disasters doing it, and India now owns design authority over a modern combat aircraft — the airframe, the flight control laws, increasingly the radar — which no amount of buying abroad would ever have given it. The cost of the delay buys a capability that compounds into Mark 2 and into the fifth-generation AMCA. The case for urgency: squadrons are falling now, the threat does not wait for a learning curve, and a programme that puts a signature on a contract in 2021 and delivers nothing by late 2026 has an accountability problem whoever is at fault. Some add that depending on an American engine for an aircraft built to end foreign dependence is the sharpest irony in Indian defence. Notice what neither side argues: nobody in this debate says the aircraft is bad. They are arguing about time. |

### Fact check before rendering

1. **"Zero handed over" is the whole cold open, and it is the most perishable line in the series so far.** As of 19 November 2026 no Mk1A handover has been announced, and The Week of 13 November 2026 reports the first may come in January 2027. **Re-check this on the day of the render.** If a handover has happened, the cold open, the Mk1A status row and the engine segment all have to be rewritten, and the episode's framing softens from "zero" to "the first ones, three years late".
2. **"Ten built" and the engine counts are reporting, not government releases**, and the narration says so each time ("HAL officials told reporters", "The Tribune reported"). Keep that attribution. Do not upgrade either to an official figure.
3. **The 2021 contract figures are PIB's**: ₹45,696 crore procurement plus ₹1,202 crore D&D and infrastructure, "close to ₹48,000 crore", 73 Mk-1A plus 10 trainers, first three in the third year then sixteen a year. The narration rounds to "forty-eight thousand crore" and quotes the delivery schedule as PIB's.
4. **The 97-aircraft contract** is ₹62,370 crore excluding taxes, signed 25 September 2025 (The Week); AIR gives "over ₹62,000 crore" and the 68/29 split, the 64% indigenous content, Uttam and Swayam Raksha Kavach. Both are consistent; the narration uses the precise figure.
5. **The 1998 embargo paragraph is attributed** ("One Indian account of that period says the withdrawal turned the clock back by four years"). The four-year figure is one publication's, not an official finding. Keep it attributed and do not state a cause-and-effect beyond it.
6. **Squadron numbers are as reported in September 2025** (31 falling to 29, against 42.5 sanctioned) and are given with that date attached. If the Air Force publishes a newer figure before the render, update it and keep the date.
7. **The Dubai crash:** the pilot is named once, with rank, as the IAF named him. No cause is stated or implied, because the court of inquiry had not reported publicly as of 19 November 2026. If findings are published before the render, say only what the inquiry says. **No aircraft is drawn in this beat** — text card only, held still.
8. **Kaveri thrust figures** (about 72 kN against 83–85 kN) are reported figures, introduced as "reports put". The 2008 delinking is stated plainly, which matches the reporting.
9. **The Kaveri Derivative Engine result is reporting, dated and attributed** ("On the first of October 2026 it was reported"). The Week's piece rests on defence sources and other press reports, with no on-record official statement, so the narration must not upgrade it to a DRDO announcement. The claim made in the narration is deliberately narrow: an Indian-designed military turbofan flew on a testbed and beat its own thrust target. It is not described as ready, inducted, or suitable for a fighter — the script says plainly "That is not an engine for Tejas."
10. **The AMCA engine partner has not been chosen.** Rolls-Royce's terms are its own pitch as reported in June 2026, not an agreed contract, and Safran is still in the running. Keep "Rolls-Royce's pitch, reported in June 2026, offers…" exactly as written; do not imply a decision. **Re-check before rendering** — if a partner is announced, this paragraph and the status row change.
11. **The 80% technology transfer on the F414 is reported**, and the narration carries the sources' own caveat that licence production is not design capability. Keep both halves; dropping the caveat would overstate it.
12. **The single-crystal blade description** is standard, publicly published engineering, at the level any aerospace textbook gives. It contains no manufacturing process detail, no alloy specification and nothing that offers uplift; keep it at that level and do not add specifics.
13. **No maps, no logos, no flags, no likenesses.** Bases (Sulur, Naliya, Nal, Nashik) and the Russian test institutes appear in narration only, never plotted.
