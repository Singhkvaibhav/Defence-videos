# India's Defence, Decoded — Episode 6: The Ship That Carries Its Own Airfield

**Working title:** *INS Vikrant: What It Takes to Build an Aircraft Carrier at Home*
**In three words:** "STEEL · DECK · WING" · **Blueprint wall:** Vikrant pinned as the sixth card · **As of:** 21 November 2026 · **Refresh by:** June 2027 (an IAC-2 contract, the first Rafale-M deliveries, or any new operational milestone)

**Length:** about 13:19, bilingual (English and Hindi audio tracks). English narration about 1,732 words across 15 segments.

**The episode's argument:** India built the ship. That is the part everyone assumed India could not do, and it turned out to be the part India could do — down to making the steel, because nobody would sell it. What India has not yet built is the thing the ship exists to carry. A carrier is a hull, a deck and an air wing; India now makes the first, has earned the second, and still buys the third. This is the same shape as Episode 5's engine, one layer up, and the episode says so once.

**Safety and accuracy notes:**
- **No maps.** Kochi, Karwar and Visakhapatnam are named in narration only, never plotted. No sea lanes, no deployment areas, no positions.
- **No operational detail.** Nothing on where the ship goes, what it carries, how it is escorted, or what its sensors do. Everything here is from PIB releases, Navy statements and published reporting.
- **1971 is stated plainly and briefly**, as the old ship's service history, with no imagery of strikes, casualties or a blockade in action. The bell is the object on screen, not the war.
- **IAC-2 is reported, not announced.** The 2023 in-principle clearance is attributed to reporting; the script says no contract had been announced publicly as of 21 November 2026, and the fact-check list flags it to re-verify.
- **No logos, flags or likenesses.** Officials appear as names in text only.

| 2:04–3:09 | The identity card | A side elevation of a carrier draws itself on, ski-jump last. Spec rows print beside it one at a time. | Start with what it is. INS Vikrant, commissioned at Kochi on the second of September 2022. Two hundred and sixty-two metres long, sixty-two metres across, about forty-three thousand tonnes fully loaded, twenty-eight knots flat out, seven and a half thousand nautical miles of range. Inside, around two thousand two hundred compartments and a crew of about sixteen hundred, with a hospital that has operating theatres, an intensive care unit and a CT scanner, because at sea there is nowhere to send anyone. It carries up to thirty aircraft. It was designed by the Navy's own Warship Design Bureau and built by Cochin Shipyard, with about seventy-six per cent indigenous content, more than a hundred small and medium firms in the supply chain, and roughly thirteen thousand people working on it in ancillary industry. It is the largest warship ever built in India. |
| 3:09–4:28 | The steel | A steel plate draws in and is stamped SHIP-GRADE. A card beside it reads WARSHIP-GRADE STEEL · SAIL · DRDO · INDIAN NAVY. | Now the part that almost no one outside the industry talks about. You cannot build a warship out of ordinary ship steel. A warship's hull has to take shock — the whip of a near-miss transmitted through water — without cracking, and it has to be weldable in enormous quantities by ordinary yard workers. That is a specific grade, and in the 2000s India did not make it. It was imported, and the supply was not something India controlled. So the Defence Research and Development Organisation, the Steel Authority of India and the Navy developed the grade at home. The Press Information Bureau's language is worth quoting exactly: the work made India "self-sufficient" in warship-grade steel. Think about what that means in sequence. Before India could build the ship, India had to build the ability to make the metal the ship is made of. That is why these programmes take the time they take, and it is also why they are worth more than the one ship at the end of them. |
| 4:28–5:24 | Seventeen years | A horizontal timeline prints marker by marker: APR 2005 STEEL CUT · FEB 2009 KEEL LAID · AUG 2013 LAUNCHED · NOV 2020 BASIN TRIALS · AUG 2021 SEA TRIALS · 28 JUL 2022 DELIVERED · 2 SEP 2022 COMMISSIONED. | The build, then. Seventeen years from the first cut of steel. That is long, and some of it was avoidable. It is also roughly what carriers take in countries that have built them for a century, and India was building the yard capability and the design skill at the same time as the ship. |
| 5:24–6:42 | Why there is a ramp | A side view of the deck. An aircraft accelerates along it and the deck curves up at the end; a dotted arc shows the climb. Beside it, a second diagram shows a catapult track, greyed, labelled NOT FITTED. Tags: STOBAR · SKI-JUMP · THREE ARRESTER WIRES. | Look at the front of the ship and you will see the deck curve upwards, like a skateboard ramp. That is a ski-jump, and it is there because Vikrant has no catapult. A catapult — steam or electromagnetic — grabs an aircraft and throws it off the deck, which lets it leave heavy, with full fuel and a full weapons load. India does not have that technology, and the countries that do have been slow to share it. So Vikrant uses what is called STOBAR: Short Take-Off But Arrested Recovery. The jet runs down the deck under its own engine power, the ramp flings it upwards, and it climbs away while it builds up speed. Coming back, it catches one of three arrester wires. It works. The honest cost of it is that an aircraft leaving a ski-jump cannot be as heavy as one leaving a catapult, which means less fuel, or fewer weapons, or both. Every carrier in the world is a set of compromises, and that is Vikrant's. |
| 6:42–7:38 | The deck, which is a skill | The deck from above. A dotted approach path curves in; three wire lines flash in turn. Dates print: 6 FEB 2023 FIRST FIXED-WING LANDINGS · 24 MAY 2023 FIRST NIGHT LANDING. | So, the second problem: the deck. On the sixth of February 2023, a MiG-29K made the first fixed-wing landing on Vikrant, and the indigenous Light Combat Aircraft, Navy version, landed the same day. On the twenty-fourth of May 2023, a MiG-29K landed on her at night. The Navy called it a historic milestone, and that is not a press-release flourish. A night landing on a carrier means flying onto a dark, moving, pitching rectangle with almost no visual cues, at an approach speed where there is no time to think. Navies measure themselves on it. Within three months of the first daylight landing, Indian pilots were doing it in the dark, on a ship India had just built. That is problem two, solved. |
| 7:38–8:52 | The wing, which is not | The small aircraft icon from the anchor, still an outline. Cards print: MiG-29K · IMPORTED and 26 RAFALE-M · 28 APR 2025 · DELIVERY BY 2030, then TEDBF · IN DEVELOPMENT. | Which leaves the third. The aircraft. Vikrant's fighters are MiG-29Ks, which are Russian. On the twenty-eighth of April 2025, India and France signed an inter-governmental agreement for twenty-six Rafale-Marine aircraft — twenty-two single-seat and four twin-seat — with deliveries completed by 2030. The package includes training, simulators, weapons, a Rafale fuselage production facility in India, maintenance and overhaul facilities for engines, sensors and weapons here, and technology transfer to integrate Indian weapons on the aircraft. That is a serious industrial deal, and it is still a purchase. India's own answer is the Twin Engine Deck Based Fighter, being developed for exactly this job, and it is years away. So: India built the ship, India can fly from the ship, and the thing that flies is bought. If that sounds like the Tejas story one deck higher, it is the same story. The hull is the easy part to be proud of. The things that go on it are where dependence lives. |
| 8:52–9:49 | Why a carrier at all | A simple diagram: a land airbase with a fixed radius circle, beside a ship with a radius circle that moves. No coastlines, no borders. | Step back and ask the obvious question: why spend forty-three thousand tonnes and two decades on this at all? Because an air force flying from land can only reach so far, and the runway stays where it is. A carrier is an airfield that moves — one that can be somewhere the day after a decision rather than the year after a treaty. For a country with ocean on three sides and interests that run from the Gulf to the Malacca Strait, that reach is the argument. A carrier also does the quiet work: disaster relief, evacuations, showing up. Much of what a navy does in peacetime is simply being present, and a carrier is the most visible way of being present that exists. |
| 9:49–10:52 | Where people disagree | Two equal cards side by side, headed THE CASE FOR CARRIERS and THE CASE AGAINST, filling in line by line. | And here, as with every big defence choice, informed people genuinely disagree, and the disagreement is worth understanding rather than winning. The case for carriers: reach that nothing else gives you, air cover for a fleet far from home, and an industrial base that only exists because you keep building ships. The case against: a carrier is a very large object in an era of very long-range anti-ship missiles, it needs a flotilla of escorts to protect it, and the money could buy a great many submarines, which are hard to find by design. Submarines hide; carriers are seen, and being seen is precisely their purpose, which is their strength and their exposure in the same sentence. Navies around the world are having this argument. India is having it too, and it is not settled anywhere. |
| 10:52–11:56 | The third ship | Two ship outlines side by side, solid, labelled VIKRAMADITYA and VIKRANT, and a third drawn as a dotted outline labelled IAC-2 · REPORTED ~45,000 t. | Which brings us to what comes next. India operates two carriers: Vikramaditya, bought from Russia and rebuilt, and Vikrant. The Navy has long said it wants three, so that two can be available while one is in refit — because a carrier spends a serious part of its life in dock, and a navy with two carriers does not always have two carriers. A second indigenous carrier, IAC-2, was reported in November 2023 to have in-principle clearance from the Defence Procurement Board, at around forty-five thousand tonnes and about forty thousand crore rupees, as a repeat order of Vikrant rather than a bigger, catapult-equipped ship. ### Fact check before rendering

1. **"No IAC-2 contract announced" is the most perishable line.** Re-check on the day of the render. If a contract or Defence Acquisition Council clearance has been announced, the third-ship beat and the status row both change, and the episode gains a date.
2. **The 2023 in-principle clearance, the 45,000-tonne figure and the ₹40,000 crore estimate are reporting**, attributed in the narration to a November 2023 report. Do not upgrade them to an official announcement. The Tribune's own wording is "top government sources".
3. **Displacement:** PIB says about 43,000 tonnes full load. Press reporting often says 45,000. The narration uses PIB's figure for Vikrant and the reported 45,000 only for IAC-2, which is how the sources have it.
4. **Cost of Vikrant is deliberately not spoken.** The widely used ₹20,000 crore figure is not in the PIB release, and official figures have been given in different forms at different times; nothing in the episode needs it.
5. **The steel claim** uses PIB's own word, "self-sufficient", quoted as a quotation. The account of why warship steel is a distinct grade is general engineering, at the level any naval architecture primer gives; keep it there and add no specification, composition or process detail.
6. **The ski-jump explanation stays at textbook level** — ramp, engine power, arrester wires, and the weight trade-off. No launch bulletins, deck cycle timings, aircraft weights or performance figures.
7. **The night-landing significance is the Navy's own framing** ("historic milestone", PIB). The explanation of why night traps are hard is general aviation, not Indian procedure.
8. **1971 appears once**, as the old ship's history, and the visual is the bell, never a depiction of the war. No strike or casualty imagery anywhere in the episode.
9. **Rafale-M value is not spoken.** PIB gave no contract value; the ₹63,000 crore figure is press reporting, and the episode does not need it. If it is added later, attribute it.
10. **No maps.** Kochi, the Gulf and the Malacca Strait are spoken, never plotted. The "reach" diagram in the Why a carrier beat is an abstract pair of circles with no coastline, landmass or boundary.
