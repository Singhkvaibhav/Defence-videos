# India's Defence, Decoded — Episode 2: BrahMos

**In three words:** "FAST · LOW · ALONE" · **Blueprint wall:** BrahMos pinned as the second card · **As of:** 13 November 2026 · **Refresh by:** May 2027 (new export contracts and the BrahMos-NG first flight)

**Length:** about 11 minutes, bilingual (English and Hindi audio tracks). English narration about 1,470 words.

**Audience:** defence enthusiasts and curious general viewers. No exam framing. Sharp explainer tone: spec moments, a real comparison chart, the combat record *and* the accident record, and the export story.

**Safety and accuracy notes:**
- **Public facts only.** Official statements, published specifications, and reported deals labelled as reports. No targeting or deployment specifics.
- **Operation Sindoor is handled carefully.** The Defence Ministry has not published a weapons list. The narration gives what is on the record (the Defence Minister's statements at Lucknow) and labels the rest as media reports.
- **The 2022 accidental firing is included**, with India's explanation and Pakistan's response both stated as each side's position. This is the trust-building moment of the episode.
- **No maps with boundaries.** Export customers appear as named pins on a schematic board of coasts and sea lanes.
- **No real likenesses.** Officials are generic figures with nameplates.

BrahMos is built on a Russian anti-ship missile, the P-800 Oniks, with Russian ramjet propulsion married to Indian guidance, software and airframe work. That shortcut is why it arrived so fast. The first test flight came on the twelfth of June 2001, from a stationary launcher at Chandipur, on the Odisha coast. The Navy inducted it in 2005. The Army followed in 2007. And in January 2020, the Air Force commissioned its first squadron of Sukhoi-30s modified to carry it. |
| 2:50–3:40 | Capability 1: speed | Two missiles race across the screen against a clock: an airliner-speed cruise missile and BrahMos. A defender's "REACTION TIME" bar shrinks from minutes to seconds. | So what does it actually do? Three things, and they work together. One: speed. BrahMos cruises at close to three times the speed of sound — and here's the part that matters: it does that for the whole flight, not just the last few seconds. Against a subsonic missile, a ship's defences might have a couple of minutes to react. Against BrahMos, they have seconds. |
| 3:40–4:25 | Capability 2: flying low | A curved horizon. A radar beam from a ship sweeps above the curve; the missile runs underneath it at 10 m, only appearing at the last moment. "RADAR HORIZON" label. | Two: it flies low. In its final run, BrahMos can come down to around ten metres above the sea. And the sea is curved. A ship's radar looks out in a straight line, so it simply cannot see something that low until it is already close. By the time the missile appears over the horizon, there is almost no time left. |
| 4:25–5:10 | Capability 3: nobody is flying it | Guidance phases light up along the flight path: INERTIAL + SATELLITE → ACTIVE RADAR SEEKER. The launch crew drives away. A target cross with a 1 m ring. | Three: nobody is flying it. BrahMos is what engineers call fire-and-forget. Once it leaves the launcher, it navigates by its own inertial system and satellite signals, then switches on its own radar for the final approach. The crew that fired it can turn around and leave. Its makers claim it lands within about a metre of the aim point, carrying a conventional warhead of two hundred to three hundred kilograms. |

| 5:40–6:30 | The range story | A gate labelled "MTCR · 300 KM / 500 KG" blocks a bar. Then "INDIA JOINS · 2016" and the gate lifts. Bars grow: 290 → 450 → 800 km, the last marked "REPORTED · IN TRIALS". | Now, range — and this is where politics shaped the engineering. For years, BrahMos flew about two hundred and ninety kilometres. Not because of physics, but because of a treaty. The Missile Technology Control Regime restricts exports of missiles that can carry a five-hundred-kilogram payload further than three hundred kilometres, and Russia is a member. India was not. In 2016, India joined, and the ceiling lifted. Longer versions followed, tested to around four hundred and fifty kilometres, and an eight-hundred-kilometre version has been reported in trials. Export customers still get the three-hundred-kilometre class. The treaty didn't go away. India just moved to the other side of it. |
| 6:30–7:30 | Is there anything like it? | A speed-versus-range chart builds: dots plot TOMAHAWK (far, slow), HARPOON and EXOCET (near, slow), KALIBR and YJ-18 (far, slow with a terminal sprint arrow), BRAHMOS (near, fast). | So, is there anything like it? Let's line them up. America's Tomahawk flies between one thousand two hundred and fifty and two thousand five hundred kilometres — far further than BrahMos. But it's subsonic, roughly the speed of an airliner. The Harpoon and France's Exocet are subsonic too, and much shorter-legged. Russia's Kalibr and China's YJ-18 do something clever in between: they cruise subsonic to save fuel, then sprint supersonic in the final seconds. See the pattern? Almost every cruise missile trades speed for range. Fuel is finite, and going fast burns it. BrahMos makes the opposite trade: it accepts a shorter reach in order to be fast the whole way. |
| 7:30–8:00 | So is it unique? | A verdict card: "UNIQUE? NOT QUITE." then "RARE? YES." then "INDIA'S TO SELL. THAT'S WHAT CHANGED." A Zircon tag sits above, marked HYPERSONIC. | So, unique? Not quite. Russia's Zircon is faster still — hypersonic, a different class altogether. And the Oniks that BrahMos grew from is still a Russian missile in its own right. But supersonic-all-the-way cruise missiles in service anywhere are rare. And BrahMos is the one that is now built in India, improved in India, and sold by India. That last part is what changed everything. |
| 8:00–8:50 | The accident | A dated card: "9 MARCH 2022". A flight path crosses a line labelled only "INTERNATIONAL BORDER — NOT DRAWN TO SCALE" as a plain arrow. Two equal statement cards: "INDIA SAYS" and "PAKISTAN SAYS". | BrahMos has a combat record. It also has an accident record, and both are worth knowing. On the ninth of March 2022, a BrahMos was fired by mistake. It flew into Pakistani territory and came down near Mian Channu, in Punjab province. It carried no warhead, and nobody was hurt. India's Defence Ministry called it an accidental firing caused by a technical malfunction during routine maintenance, and said it deeply regretted the incident. In August 2022, three Air Force officers were dismissed. Pakistan called India's explanation inadequate and asked for a joint investigation, which did not happen. It remains the only publicly known case of one nuclear-armed state accidentally firing a missile into another. |
| 8:50–9:40 | In war | Two cards side by side: "ON THE RECORD" (the Defence Minister's statements, dated) and "REPORTED, NOT OFFICIAL" (media claims about numbers of missiles and bases), with the second visibly greyed. | Then, in May 2025, came Operation Sindoor. India struck targets in Pakistan on the night of the ninth and tenth of May. Now, be careful here. The Defence Ministry has not published a detailed list of the weapons used, and the reports you may have seen naming exact numbers of missiles and airbases are media accounts, not official ones. What is on the record is this: five months later, standing inside the BrahMos factory at Lucknow, the Defence Minister said that every inch of Pakistan is within the missile's reach, and that the operation had been just a trailer. Read that how you like. It is an unusually direct statement from a government about one of its own weapons. |

Manila signed in January 2022, for about three hundred and seventy-five million dollars: shore-based anti-ship batteries for its Marine Corps. The first batch arrived on the nineteenth of April 2024 — the first time India had exported a major missile system to anybody. A second battery followed in April 2025. Since then, Vietnam: India's Defence Secretary confirmed an order at the Shangri-La Dialogue in May 2026. And Indonesia signed in July 2026, for a figure reported at between two hundred and three hundred and fifty million dollars. |

In February 2025, The Tribune reported, citing unnamed sources, that the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Egypt and Vietnam had all shown interest, mostly in a land-based version. Those are reports, not contracts. But notice where the actual buyers are. The Philippines, Vietnam, Indonesia — all three sit on the South China Sea. A fast anti-ship missile on your own coastline is a very specific answer to a very specific problem. |
| 11:05–11:45 | What's next | Lucknow plant stats count up: 200 ACRES · ₹380 CRORE · ~100 SYSTEMS A YEAR. Then a size comparison: BrahMos 8.4 m / 3,000 kg shrinking to BrahMos-NG 6 m / under 1,500 kg, beside a Tejas silhouette. | What's coming? Two things. The Lucknow plant, flagged off in October 2025, covers two hundred acres, cost about three hundred and eighty crore rupees, and is expected to turn out around a hundred missile systems a year. That is a supply line, not a showpiece. And then there's BrahMos-NG — next generation. Smaller: about six metres instead of eight and a half, and under fifteen hundred kilograms instead of three thousand. That halving matters, because a lighter missile fits on more aircraft, including the Tejas, and more of them fit on each one. BrahMos Aerospace says the first flight test is expected around 2026, with production from 2027 or 2028. A hypersonic version, BrahMos-Two, has been talked about for years, but it remains a concept rather than a programme with hardware in the air. |
