| 1:40–3:10 | How it flies | Three flight paths draw side by side: BALLISTIC (a tall arc, with a dotted "predicted" landing point), CRUISE (a low, slow line), QUASI-BALLISTIC (a flat arc that kinks late). A radar dish tracks each; the prediction line snaps when Pralay turns. | Here's what makes Pralay special. There are three ways to fly a missile to a target. One: ballistic. Throw it high, let it fall along a tall arc. It's fast, but predictable. Once a radar sees that arc, a defence system can calculate where it will land and send an interceptor to meet it. Two: cruise. It flies low, like a small jet, hugging the ground. It's hard to spot, but much slower. And three: quasi-ballistic, which is Pralay. It launches like a ballistic missile, but flies a lower, flatter arc, and in the final stage it can change course. The radar's prediction says it lands here, and then it doesn't. Defence sources told ThePrint it was designed to evade interceptor missiles, and a defence scientist quoted after the latest trial said it flies at hypersonic speed, more than five times the speed of sound. |
| 3:10–3:45 | Shoot and scoot | Cutaway of the launcher: it drives, stops, the canister rises, a "READY" timer, launch, the truck pulls away. Comparison panel: an older missile being fuelled vs a sealed tube. | Now look at the launcher. Older missiles that ran on liquid fuel had to be fuelled before firing, which took time and could give the game away. Pralay's solid fuel is already inside, sealed in its canister. The truck drives in, stops, raises the tube, fires, and moves on. Soldiers call it shoot and scoot. A launcher that never stays in one place is far harder to find, and far harder to hit back. |
| 3:45–5:00 | The road to Pralay | A timeline rail with markers lighting up: 1990s PRITHVI · 2011 PRAHAAR · 2015 PROJECT SANCTIONED · 2020 LAC FACE-OFF · DEC 2021 FIRST TESTS · NOV 2023 · 26 JAN 2025 PARADE · JUL 2025 TWIN TESTS · 5 NOV 2026 USER TRIAL. | Pralay didn't come from nowhere. India's first ballistic missile, Prithvi, joined the forces more than thirty years ago. In 2011, DRDO first tested a smaller tactical missile called Prahaar, and Pralay grew out of that programme. The project was sanctioned in 2015, with a budget of about three hundred and thirty-three crore rupees. The first two test flights came on the twenty-second and twenty-third of December 2021. Meanwhile, the clashes with China along the Line of Actual Control in mid-2020 had changed the Army's thinking, and in 2022 it asked for Pralay. Another test followed on the seventh of November 2023. On the twenty-sixth of January 2025, Pralay rolled down Kartavya Path in the Republic Day parade. On the twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth of July 2025, two back-to-back shots checked its longest and shortest range. And on the fifth of November 2026 came the user trial, where the armed forces themselves put the system through its paces. Reports say orders have been cleared for both the Army and the Air Force. |

Side panel: "CONVENTIONAL" (Pralay) vs "NUCLEAR · STRATEGIC FORCES COMMAND" (Agni). | So why does India need it? Picture a ladder of reach. At the bottom: artillery and rockets like Pinaka, reaching tens of kilometres. At the top: the Agni missiles, reaching from seven hundred to eight thousand kilometres, part of India's strategic deterrent. In between, there was a gap. Pralay fills it: a conventional missile that can hit airfields, radar sites and command centres hundreds of kilometres away, in minutes. And that word, conventional, matters. DRDO describes Pralay as a conventional missile, and India's nuclear weapons stay with a separate command, the Strategic Forces Command. Pralay is meant for the battlefield, not for nuclear deterrence. |
| 5:55–6:45 | Head to head | Duel card 1: PRALAY vs BRAHMOS, with matched rows (PATH: quasi-ballistic arc / low cruise · EDGE: speed / agility and stealth). Start at home, with BrahMos, India's famous supersonic cruise missile. BrahMos skims low and can weave towards its target. Pralay climbs and dives, and is faster. Defence sources told ThePrint that Pralay's edge is speed, while BrahMos brings agility and stealth. They aren't rivals; they're a team. Abroad, Pralay's closest cousins are Russia's Iskander, used in the war in Ukraine, China's Dong Feng-12, America's Precision Strike Missile and South Korea's Hyunmoo-2: fast, mobile, accurate battlefield missiles. And India's neighbours are already investing. On the thirteenth of January 2026, Army chief General Upendra Dwivedi said, "Pakistan has established a rocket force," adding, "It is the need of the hour that we also establish such a force." |

Asset tokens (Pralay, BrahMos, Pinaka) move between the Army, Air Force and Navy columns. | Which brings us to the fight inside India's defence world: who should control missiles like Pralay? The idea of a dedicated rocket force goes back to the late General Bipin Rawat, India's first Chief of Defence Staff. Supporters, like retired Brigadier Anil Raman, argue that India's long-range strike weapons are spread across the Army, the Air Force and the Navy, and that one command would decide faster in a crisis. Critics, like retired Air Marshal Narmdeshwar Tiwari, point out that only China and Russia have created such a force, and say India should invest in modern fighter aircraft instead.
