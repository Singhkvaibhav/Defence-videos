# India's Defence, Decoded — Episode 4: The Shield

**Working title:** *How India Is Actually Building a Missile Shield | S-400, Kusha and Sudarshan Chakra*
**In three words:** "SEE · DECIDE · SHOOT" · **Blueprint wall:** the shield pinned as the fourth card · **As of:** 17 November 2026 · **Refresh by:** June 2027 (the fifth S-400 squadron, the Kusha M2 interceptor test, or any official detail on Mission Sudarshan Chakra)

**Length:** about 13 minutes, bilingual (English and Hindi audio tracks). English narration about 1,730 words, which runs to roughly 13 minutes once the Hindi track (always the longer of the two) sets each beat's length.

**The episode's argument:** the famous thing is not the thing that matters. Everyone names the S-400. What actually decides whether a shield works is the unglamorous part — the network that sees a threat, decides which of a dozen weapons should shoot it, and does that in seconds.

**Safety and accuracy notes:**
- **No maps at all**, again. Coverage is drawn as abstract nested rings on blueprint paper, never as arcs or shaded areas over territory.
- **Government performance claims are attributed as government claims.** The PIB statement that Akashteer "intercepted and neutralised every inbound projectile" is quoted as what the Press Information Bureau said, not asserted as established fact. No independent interception counts are given, because none are public.
- **No Pakistani or Chinese system is mocked or rated.** Where the PIB release makes a comparative claim, the episode does not repeat it.
- **Officially announced facts and published analysis only.** No radar performance detail, no deployment locations, no engagement specifics beyond published ranges.
- **Delivery status is given with its date** ("as of 17 November 2026") so the episode cannot silently go stale.
- **The debate is given both sides** on equal cards.

An air defence system does three jobs, in this order. It has to see something coming. It has to decide what to do about it. And then it has to shoot. Most of us only ever think about the third one, because the third one is the one with the fireworks. The first two are harder. Second thing. Forget the word dome. People say "an air defence dome", and it makes you picture a single magic bubble. That is not what anyone builds. What you build is a set of rings, one inside the other. The outermost ring carries a long-range system with very expensive missiles. Inside it is a medium ring. Inside that, a short-range ring. And right over the thing you are protecting, guns and shoulder-fired missiles. Why go to all that trouble instead of just buying the best long-range system? Two reasons. Anything the outer ring misses, the next ring in gets a turn at. And cost. You do not fire a missile worth crores at a drone worth a couple of lakhs. Each ring exists so the ring outside it does not have to waste itself. |

Inside it sits MR-SAM, a medium-range missile built jointly by DRDO and Israel Aerospace Industries, used by both the Army and the Air Force, with a published range around seventy kilometres. Then the short-range ring: Akash, which is Indian, with its newer Akash Prime version; the Israeli SPYDER; and QRSAM, a quick-reaction Indian system designed to move with an armoured column and shoot on the move. | 4:00–5:20 | The S-400 | A long-range system drawn as a launcher vehicle plus two radar vehicles, with a card beside it: CONTRACT 5 OCT 2018 · 5 SQUADRONS · ABOUT 5.43 BILLION DOLLARS. India signed for it on the fifth of October 2018: five squadrons, for about five point four three billion dollars, roughly forty thousand crore rupees. The Air Force calls it Sudarshan Chakra, and hold on to that name too, because it comes back at the end of this episode. What you are buying is not one missile. It is a package: a search radar that scans a wide area, a second radar that locks and guides, a command vehicle, and launchers carrying several different missiles for different distances. | 6:20–7:50 | Project Kusha | A blueprint sheet headed PROJECT KUSHA. Three interceptor outlines draw in, each with its own ring: M1 150 KM · M2 250 KM · M3 350–400 KM. A date stamp lands: 23 JULY 2026 · MAIDEN FLIGHT. | It is called Project Kusha. It is DRDO's long-range surface-to-air missile — India's own answer to the outer ring. The Cabinet Committee on Security cleared its development in May 2022. In September 2023 the Defence Ministry approved procurement of five squadrons for the Air Force at twenty-one thousand seven hundred crore rupees. And the design is interesting, because it is not one missile. It is three interceptors sharing one set of radars and one command system: a short one reaching about a hundred and fifty kilometres, a middle one at around two hundred and fifty, and a long one reported at three hundred and fifty to four hundred. Then, on the twenty-third of July 2026, Kusha flew for the first time, from Dr APJ Abdul Kalam Island off the Odisha coast. The Defence Ministry said the missile successfully intercepted a high-speed, high-altitude aerial target. The Defence Minister, Rajnath Singh, said that the capability to develop long-range surface-to-air missile systems is possessed only by a handful of nations. Now, one successful first flight is a beginning, not a system. There are years of testing between that and a squadron standing on alert. Officials have talked about induction towards the end of this decade. But the significance is real: the outer ring is the layer India could not build, and now it has flown one. |
| 7:50–8:50 | A different problem: ballistic missiles | A split frame. Left: an aircraft marker crossing slowly, labelled "ABOUT 900 KM/H". Right: a steep arc falling fast, labelled "SEVERAL KILOMETRES PER SECOND". A separate ring labelled BMD draws in above both. | Now a distinction that gets blurred constantly. Shooting down an aircraft or a cruise missile is one problem. Shooting down a ballistic missile is another problem altogether. An airliner travels at roughly nine hundred kilometres an hour. A ballistic warhead coming back down through the atmosphere is doing several kilometres every second. You are not tracking it; you are predicting where it will be and putting something there first. India has a separate programme for exactly this, sanctioned back in 2000. Its first phase was designed against missiles of up to the two thousand kilometre class, using two interceptors and a long-range tracking radar, and the government has said that phase has been tested and deployed. A second phase, aimed at the five thousand kilometre class, has been flight-tested from Chandipur. What is publicly known about where it is deployed is very little, and that is deliberate. |
| 8:50–10:05 | The part nobody names | Three labelled boxes draw in and are joined by lines: IACCS (AIR FORCE) · AKASHTEER (ARMY) · TRIGUN (NAVY). Sensor dots across the frame connect to all three. A clock counts down in seconds beside them. | Which brings us back to where we started, and to the thing almost nobody names. Every weapon in every ring is useless on its own, because the hard question in air defence is not "can this missile reach that target". It is: of the dozen weapons that can reach it, which one should fire, and have we already shot at it twice, and is that aircraft ours? You have seconds to answer. So India built a network. The Air Force runs a system called IACCS, the Integrated Air Command and Control System. The Army runs Akashteer. The Navy runs TRIGUN. They connect, and they share one picture of the sky. The PIB describes Akashteer as a fully indigenous, automated air defence control and reporting system, mounted on vehicles so it can move, pulling in several kinds of radar, and giving everyone a common real-time air picture. The reason that matters is unglamorous and enormous: it stops two units firing at the same drone while a third goes unengaged, and it reduces the chance of shooting at your own aircraft. A better missile makes one engagement better. A better network makes every engagement better. |

A large ring draws around an abstract cluster of small civilian shapes — a hospital cross, a rail line, a building — with the date 2035 beneath. | And on the fifteenth of August 2025, from the Red Fort, the Prime Minister announced the ambition that ties all of this together: Mission Sudarshan Chakra, a national security shield to be built out and modernised by 2035. He said the protective shield must keep expanding so that every citizen of the country feels secure, and that whatever technology may be used to attack us, our technology must prove superior. Two things about it are worth being precise on. First, it is described as both defensive and offensive — not only stopping an attack but answering it. Second, it is explicitly meant to cover civilian sites too: hospitals, railways, places of worship. Beyond that, the details have not been published, and anything you read about exactly which systems it will include is informed speculation, not announcement. |
| 10:55–11:55 | Where people disagree | Two equal cards side by side, headed THE CASE FOR and THE CASE FOR CAUTION, filling in line by line. | This is where thoughtful people disagree, and both sides are worth hearing. The case for: May 2025 showed that layered air defence works, that a cheap drone attack can be absorbed, and that the right lesson is to build more of it at home so it cannot be switched off from abroad. The case for caution: a shield invites bigger salvos, because the cheapest way past any defence is to send more than it can handle at once — and drones are cheap while interceptors are not, so the arithmetic can run against the defender. Some analysts add that visible defences can look destabilising to a neighbour, who may build more offence in response. Notice that neither argument is about whether the equipment works. They are arguments about what other people do next. |
